Swapping a tap is a small job with one big risk: water everywhere. The job is really three tasks — isolate the supply, undo the old tap, fit the new one with the right seals.

The mistake is starting before the isolation valves actually hold. They often do not, and that is when a small job becomes a floor-drying job.

Which tier to buy

Cheapest that works

A basic tap with an included fitting kit and a cheap basin wrench. Fine if the existing pipework is standard and reachable. Walmart →

Buy the good one

A tap with a ceramic cartridge and metal tails, plus a proper basin wrench. Cartridges are the part that fails first, and metal tails cross-thread far less often than plastic. Home Depot →

Tools you probably already own

Adjustable spanner, Basin wrench, Screwdriver set, Torch, Bucket and towels, Gloves. Do not buy these new for one job.

What to skip, and why

Replacing the tap without checking the isolation valves first

Old isolation valves frequently will not fully shut. Test them, and if they weep or will not close, turn the water off at the mains stopcock instead — before you undo anything.

PTFE tape on a compression fitting

PTFE is for threaded joints. On a compression fitting the olive makes the seal; wrapping tape around it usually causes leaks rather than preventing them.

Over-tightening the tail nuts to stop a drip

It crushes the washer and makes the leak worse. If it drips, undo it, check the washer is seated flat and square, then tighten firmly but not violently.

How the quantities were worked out

Measure before buying: the hole diameter in the sink deck is usually 35 mm, but it is worth checking, because a tap that does not fit the existing hole turns a 30-minute job into a sink-replacement job.

Check the reach: measure from the back of the sink to the front edge. A spout that is too short means you will be fighting the sink every time you fill a pan.

If any isolation valve does not hold, stop and isolate at the mains. A tap change is not worth a flooded kitchen floor.
