A raised bed is four boards, a base and something to keep the soil in. It goes wrong in two ways: timber that rots within two seasons, and drainage that turns the bed into a bog.

This list is sized for a 1.2 m × 2.4 m bed at 300 mm high, so you are cutting as little as possible.

Which tier to buy

Cheapest that works

Standard treated boards and a gravel base. Accept that the cheapest timber may need replacing around year three or four. Walmart →

Buy the good one

Thicker boards and proper 75 mm corner posts, screwed rather than nailed. A bed that stays square lasts several seasons longer than one that racks out of shape. Home Depot →

Tools you probably already own

Drill/driver, Circular saw or handsaw, Spirit level, Tape measure, Mallet, Wheelbarrow, Gloves. Do not buy these new for one job.

What to skip, and why

Railway sleepers for a vegetable bed

Old sleepers can be contaminated with creosote or oil. For anything you plan to eat, use timber that is sold for garden use and confirm it is safe for food-growing.

A solid plastic sheet as the base

It holds water. The base layer is for drainage, so it must be open — gravel or coarse bark, not a sealed membrane.

Filling the bed with 100% cheap topsoil

Cheap topsoil compacts into something closer to brick. Mix in compost so it holds moisture and stays workable.

How the quantities were worked out

Perimeter: a 1.2 m × 2.4 m bed is (1.2 + 2.4) × 2 = 7.2 m of board run per course. At 2400 mm per board, each course of the two long sides and two short sides takes 4 boards, so 300 mm of height is one course and 8 boards gives two courses.

Volume: 1.2 × 2.4 × 0.3 = 0.864 m³ total. Allowing 300 mm of drainage at the base and leaving a 50 mm gap at the top, the soil volume is about 1.2 × 2.4 × 0.2 = 0.58 m³, roughly 600 litres of fill.

Fill volumes are approximate because topsoil and compost settle after the first watering. Add a little at a time and top up after a week.
