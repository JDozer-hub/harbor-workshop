Most shower leaks that look like tray leaks are actually a failed joint higher up, or a blocked drain channel pushing water sideways. Seal the wrong thing and the leak comes back in a fortnight.

The list below covers the honest case: cutting out old sealant, drying properly, and re-sealing the joints that move.

What to skip, and why

Sealing over the old bead

New sealant will not bond to old cured silicone. It looks fine for a week and then peels. Cut the old bead out completely — it is most of the job for a reason.

Sealing a joint that is still damp

Silicone will not adhere to a wet surface. After cutting out, leave the joint to dry fully, and if the wall behind is soaked, stop and find the actual leak first.

Using grout where the joint needs to move

Corners and tray joints move with temperature and weight. Grout cracks there; flexible sealant does not. That is what the gap is for.

How the quantities were worked out

Silicone coverage: a standard tube lays roughly 10-12 m of a 6 mm bead. The perimeter of a typical 900 × 900 mm shower tray is 3.6 m plus the enclosure uprights, so one tube usually covers it and the second is your margin for a re-run.

Curing: most sanitary sealants need around 24 hours before the shower is used. Plan the job for a day when nobody needs the shower.

If the ceiling below the bathroom is stained, the leak is likely from the waste or the supply rather than the sealant. Sealing will not fix that.
