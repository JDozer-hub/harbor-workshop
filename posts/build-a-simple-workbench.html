A workbench fails in one specific way: it racks. Push it sideways and the whole frame turns into a parallelogram, because there is nothing resisting the movement.

The fix is cheap — a diagonal brace or a back panel — and the rest of the list is just timber and screws.

Which tier to buy

Cheapest that works

Standard structural timber and a plywood top. It will be rigid if you brace it, and it will mark easily — accept that. Walmart →

Buy the good one

A thicker bench top and bolted joints with a plywood back panel. A heavier top is what makes a bench pleasant to work on: it absorbs hammer blows instead of ringing. Home Depot →

Tools you probably already own

Circular saw, Drill/driver, Spirit level, Square, Tape measure, Clamps, Wood glue brush. Do not buy these new for one job.

What to skip, and why

Skipping the back panel or diagonal brace

This is the single decision that determines whether the bench is usable. Four legs and a top with square joints racks immediately under sideways load.

A chipboard or MDF top for a workbench

It swells and crumbles the first time it gets wet or takes a hammer blow. Plywood or solid timber is not much more expensive and lasts far longer.

Nailing the frame instead of screwing and gluing it

Nails pull out under vibration. Screws with glue, or bolts at the leg joints, are what keep a bench tight for years.

How the quantities were worked out

Timber: four legs at 900 mm and four rails totalling 4.8 m for an 1800 mm bench comes to about 8.4 m of timber. At 2400 mm per length, that is 4 lengths, plus 2 for the aprons and the brace — 6 in total.

Bench height: most people are comfortable with a bench top around wrist height when standing, commonly 850-900 mm. Measure rather than copying a number — a bench at the wrong height is a bench you avoid using.

Cut and drill in a ventilated space, and check for nails or screws before cutting any reclaimed timber.
